Recently I've started questioning my leadership methods more often. Leading has always felt natural to me, but I've never been able to explain my methods or leadership style. You would think every team I've led is different, since I don't have a "method", yet all the teams I've led in the last 6 years have one thing in common: a strong culture and sense of community. (And a mascot!)

In this post, I'll try to put into words how I created this culture in my team at G2i, and hopefully answer the question of "how I did it" for the other teams I've led in the past.

### Be one of them
"Well yes, I am one of them; it's my team. What a silly point."

Well, let me explain it. When you become a manager, everything changes. Your responsibilities shift so much that you'll eventually find yourself moving further away from coding and closer to people/project management. If it is your first time managing, then it's even worse because, on top of new priorities, you also have to learn what it is to be a manager and what is expected of you.

It's so easy to get buried in the new priorities, meetings, and planning that you forget about your team members. You only see them in standup or when they need your help, and you realize you're not really active with the team or connecting with them. That's not "being one of them"; that's being a boss.

Now don't get me wrong. There's nothing wrong with being a boss. Sometimes you'll have to. But if you only show up when you're needed, or when someone did something wrong and you have to "correct" them, then your team doesn't see you as an equal.

Being one of them means connecting with the team and the individual members. Share the same pains and the same victories. In a football team (soccer for Americans), the captain has many additional responsibilities, but they're still one of the team. They train together, laugh together, win together, and lose together. Fun fact: the captain is usually the only team member who plays the entire game (besides the keeper). That is who you need to be.

#### My personal example
I'm leading a team of 5 engineers. My responsibilities are project and people management, reports, and planning. I rarely have time to code, but I do my best to push one or two PRs a week. The way I have found to be the best to connect with my team is through feature planning and code reviews. I engage with them frequently in the Slack channels for feature planning and QA, and I try to unblock them with code reviews whenever possible.

This helps me keep up with them, especially in their day-to-day work. I rarely need to wait for someone to tell me something's wrong, because I'm actively there all the time to see it. CI is failing for everyone? Yes, I know; I'm code reviewing and seeing it in red. GitHub is down again? Yup, saw it when I couldn't merge this PR.

These things, combined with the next topic, keep me always engaged with the team, even if I'm not doing the same type of work they do.

### Team Bonding
I know some of you might not like spending time with your coworkers. It's understandable. You have a lot of work to do, and engaging with others won't get your tickets done; maybe it feels forced and unnatural. Yet, as a leader, if you want to build a trustworthy, strong culture, you **need** to get yourself out of the comfort zone.

So, to keep it from feeling forced, use the time you already booked with them!

It's not every day that you need a detailed round of reports from your engineers. If you do a good job at ticket organization and constant communication, stand-ups tend to be the space only for blockers and plans for the day / next day. Use this in your favor.

If your stand-up is 15 minutes and you only need 10, use the remaining 5 to talk and engage with your team. I like to start by saying something that happened to me - like when I got my new split keyboard and couldn't stop talking about it for weeks. The conversation unfolds on its own after that.

You will also need to make people engage with each other when you're not around, and that is the hard part. Your team must not be dependent on you to keep the machine running, and to do that, they must have a good relationship with each other. Now, no one likes to be forced to participate in a happy hour, but there are easier ways to get people to work together: Duos.

Organizing your tickets and features so two people can work on them together is the best way I've found to get my team to interact. By frequently changing the duos, I get everyone to work with each other at least once, and by working on the same thing and going through the same pains, they build their own bond!

I've found **perfect duos** (people who low-key were born to work together) multiple times by doing this.

Combine this with encouragement for knowledge sharing, and you get a team that learns to work and help each other. You build a team that has pride in itself and enjoys working together. A happy engineer makes a happy team, and a happy team makes good deliveries.

### Leaders are the wall between the team and the world
Okay, maybe that's a little bit dramatic. I was thinking about that soldier protecting the sleeping kid meme lol.

But there is some truth to that. As a manager, you're supposed to protect your engineers and ensure they have all rocks taken out of their way, so they can focus on what really matters: shipping.

> "The responsibility of keeping your teams successfully moving forward and happily engaged is on your shoulders." - The Manager's Path, Camille Fournier.

Note that it does not mean keeping them in the dark about the problems that are happening around them. Open, honest communication from you will be met with the same from the team. But knowing about a problem doesn't mean they need to be the one to fix it.

I've always seen my role as a leader as the fine line between delegation and enabling focus. It is very important that you learn the ability to prioritize tasks, because with that you'll know what is so important that you'll need to delegate to someone who can deeply focus on it; or what you can do yourself and keep your team from the burden of taking yet another request that will break their focus.

Delegating is important, but enabling your team to have proper focus sessions will help them focus on their deliveries and, quite often - as I've come to realize - remove stress from context switching and overwork. If you're an engineer reading this, you'll know that nothing is more stressful than having to context-switch without being allowed proper focus time.

All this to say that, whenever possible, take a hit for them. If something is quick and easy to do, do it yourself. If a new request comes in but your team is already running out of time to finish everything for next week's delivery, consider taking the new request so your team can focus on the most urgent priorities. Even better: deny the request! (When possible)

If you help your team and lead by example, they'll help you back. A team is all about helping each other succeed with a common goal in mind. Your job is to be the main person doing that. Eventually, others will follow.

### But it will take time
Culture is not something you build overnight. It took me months of work, project rotation, and engineers coming and going to finally build a culture that I was proud of. A team I was proud to be a part of.

Be patient and kind. Each engineer will have their own time to engage. Some are shy and take weeks; some are extroverted and are part of the culture from day one. Organic culture - the one that really engraves in a team - can only be built with patience.

### Conclusion
If the team feels like they can count on each other, and count on you, everything else follows smoothly given the proper time. Engage with your team and build a strong sense of community (internally in the team and externally!), and watch your team care about the project and each other.

And maybe, if you talk about things other than work, you'll eventually find something the team likes so much that it becomes an internal joke and, eventually, a mascot 🦀.
 
Make it personal; you spend way too much time with these people for it to be "just work".
